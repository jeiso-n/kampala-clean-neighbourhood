About the Kampala Clean Neighbourhood Initiative

A university civic-technology initiative addressing urban solid waste management challenges in the Greater Kampala Metropolitan Area.

Academic Context: Developed for Victoria University Kampala course Internet Technology & Web Design (1315 ST). The mission of this project is to apply semantic web standards, accessibility principles, and client-side web technologies to solve real urban civic challenges.

Our Vision & Objectives

The Kampala Clean Neighbourhood platform connects residents, registered private waste collectors, and community leaders. We aim to:

  • Democratise Timetable Data: Make division and parish collection days transparent to eliminate missed waste pickups.
  • Verify Collection Concessionaires: Help households identify officially licensed operators to combat illegal fly-tipping.
  • Facilitate Grassroots Incident Reporting: Provide an accessible, photo-capable reporting interface for reporting blocked drains and overflowing skips.
  • Promote Separation at Source: Educate households on separating biodegradable food scraps from recyclable plastics and paper.

Institutional & Legal Framework

Solid waste management in Kampala operates under the legal mandate of the Kampala City Council (Solid Waste Management) Ordinance and the National Environment Act (2019) administered by the National Environment Management Authority (NEMA).

Under these regulations, KCCA divides the city into operational zones serviced by municipal fleets alongside private concessionaires licensed under public-private partnership agreements.

Urban Research & Data References

Kampala Capital City Authority (KCCA)

Annual Waste Characterisation Assessments indicate Kampala produces approximately 0.5 to 0.6 kg of solid waste per capita per day, creating an aggregate volume exceeding 28,000 tonnes monthly.

National Environment Management Authority (NEMA)

National State of the Environment Reports consistently identify uncontrolled burning and drainage dumping in wetland buffers (such as Lubigi and Nakivubo) as major risks to Lake Victoria's ecosystem.

Makerere University Department of Environmental Management

Academic waste composition studies note that over 75% of Kampala's waste consists of wet biodegradable organics (matooke peels, vegetable tops, crop market residuals), making decentralised composting viable.
